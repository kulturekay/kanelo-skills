# Hiring & Training Your Team

> **Service Delivery · File 4 (Team).**
> *Managing Team Execution* (File 3) described the pod that delivers and the rhythm that keeps it moving. This file is one level back: **how you build that team in the first place** — finding, filtering, hiring, onboarding, and developing the people who do (and lead) the work.
>
> **AI-native note — read this one carefully.** Unlike the rest of Service Delivery, hiring and training *people* is the part that is **least automatable and most human**. An AI-native agency doesn't escape hiring — it changes *who* it hires: fewer, higher-leverage humans who hold the judgement, taste, accountability, and relationship gates (the Brand Lead / Creative Director / Commercial Lead roles from File 3 that should *not* be automated). So this file has two readings: (1) the literal one — how to hire and train humans, which stands on its own; and (2) a genuine structural parallel — the same disciplined process (spec → task → trial → onboard → review) is almost exactly how you'd safely "hire and train" an *agent* too. Where the parallel is real, it's flagged; where hiring is just human, it's left human. **All roles, JDs, brands, and scorecards below are illustrative examples** — the transferable asset is the process.

The core idea: **hiring is a filtering process, and the best filter is a task, not a CV.** Talk is cheap and CVs are polished; the agency model here front-loads *demonstrated ability* (a real task) before investing interview time, and de-risks the final commitment with a paid trial before a permanent contract. Get the process right and you reliably acquire people who can actually do the work; get it wrong and you inherit the failure modes from File 3 (missed deadlines, burnout, dissatisfaction) at the root.

---

## 1. The hiring process — 7 steps

A linear funnel from "we need someone" to "they're on the team," each step a filter:

| # | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Job Description** | Draft from the template — about you, the role, expectations. |
| 2 | **Advertise Job** | Post on the relevant job board(s); use an application **form** to shortlist so you're not reading every CV — weed out those who don't meet criteria. |
| 3 | **Task** | Ask candidates to complete a task that **demonstrates their ability to do the actual job.** |
| 4 | **Shortlisting** | Shortlist *based on the execution of the task* (not the CV). |
| 5 | **Interview** | Assess communication style, confidence, and ask deeper questions about the role + their experience. |
| 6 | **Trial** | A **2-week trial period** to confirm work ethic + output in reality. |
| 7 | **Hire + Contract** | Passed the trial → hire and draft an official contract. |

> **The design principle:** every step removes risk before the next, bigger investment. The form filters before you read CVs; the **task** filters before you spend interview time; the **trial** filters before you sign a contract. The task is the heart of it — it inverts the usual order (interview-then-maybe-test) so that *only people who can already do the work* reach the interview.

## 2. The Job Description (Step 1)

A good JD is structured, specific, and free of fluff. The template's sections:

- **Who we are / about the agency** — exciting context to entice applicants and sell the growth journey (the example name-drops marquee clients to signal credibility).
- **The role** — 1–2 sentences on the role, what you're looking for, and why.
- **Contract type + working hours** — e.g. full-time freelance, 9–6 GMT (set the timezone expectation explicitly for remote roles).
- **Role & responsibilities** — *specific, real tasks, no fluff* — what they'll actually do day to day.
- **Ideal skills + experience** — minimum years, prior-role experience, specific software/systems, other skills.
- **What to expect** — pace of the agency, timezone, the support they'll get, the team they're joining.
- **How to apply** — *complete the task below and send to [email].*

> **The pattern across every example JD** (Finance Assistant, Junior Video Editor, Sales Copywriter, Paid Social Manager, Lead Gen Executive): responsibilities are concrete verbs tied to tools (e.g. "daily bank reconciliations on Xero," "edit social-first video in Premiere Pro/After Effects," "source inspiration in Foreplay.io," "keep the CRM updated daily"). Specificity in the JD is itself a filter — it self-selects people who recognise the actual work.

> **AI-native parallel:** a JD is structurally identical to an **agent role/system definition** — responsibilities = the agent's scope of tools and tasks, "ideal skills + experience" = required capabilities/model choice, "what to expect" = operating context. Writing a tight JD and writing a tight agent spec are the same discipline: define the real tasks and the tools precisely, no fluff.

## 3. The Task (Steps 2–4) — the core filter

Every example role ships with a **role-specific task** that mirrors the real job:

| Role | The task |
|---|---|
| **Junior Video Editor** | Use supplied podcast footage to produce 2× ≤30s TikToks that maximise engagement/reach and click-through to the podcast. |
| **Sales Copywriter** | Draft Facebook ad copy + a sales landing page for a given client, taking the reader from ad → sales page → purchase/book-a-call. |
| **Paid Social Manager** | Draft copy for 2× video ads for a given client and campaign aim, as you'd brief the team to create them. |
| **Lead Gen Executive** | **Source:** pinpoint 3 prospects for the agency and explain why. **Email:** write an outreach email to a current client explaining why they need the service. |

> **Why the task works:** it's a *work sample* — the single best predictor of job performance. It also doubles as a filter on effort and comprehension (who follows the brief, who goes above it). For roles that produce assets, the task *is* a portfolio piece for the exact work you need.

> **AI-native parallel:** the task is an **eval.** "Give the candidate a representative job and score the output" is exactly how you'd qualify an agent for a workflow — a held-out, real-world test case with a scoring rubric. The interview scorecard (§5) is the rubric.

## 4. Where to advertise (Step 2)

Match the board to the role and budget. The most-used:

| Board | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **OnlineJobs.ph** | VAs, lead-gen execs, sales/CS assistants, design support, bookkeeping/data entry (Filipino talent) | Free up to 3 jobs/15 apps; paid tiers ~$69–$99/mo |
| **LinkedIn** | Highly skilled remote/hybrid; *core team* roles (marketing, design leads, PMs, talent/social managers) | ~£5/day to promote; "Recruiter" package for headhunting; best-quality candidates; mainly UK/US/EU |
| **Indeed** | Junior–mid remote roles, location-targeted | Free to post + paid promotion; huge candidate pool (350M+ visitors/mo); used for devs, sales assistants in specific countries/timezones |
| **Fiverr** | One-off/project work (logos, copy, editing, SEO, SMM) | Fixed-price packages; people come, do a project, move on |
| **Upwork** | Junior staff at cheaper rates, short-term projects | Proposals-based; wide range of skills/price points |

Plus 15 more remote boards for specific needs — e.g. **Toptal** (top-3% elite freelancers), **We Work Remotely / Remote OK / Remotive** (remote-first tech & marketing), **Dribbble** (creative/design), **Overpass** (remote sales/CS teams), **AI Jobs Net** (ML/AI/data roles), **FlexJobs / Working Nomads / Guru / PeoplePerHour / Hubstaff Talent / Freelancer / Remote.co / Skip the Drive**.

> **The lesson:** different boards yield different talent tiers and contract types. Core, high-trust roles came through LinkedIn; cheap/fast project work through Fiverr/Upwork; offshore operational roles (often excellent English, working your timezone) through OnlineJobs.ph and Indeed. Pick the board that matches the role's level and permanence.

## 5. Interviewing + the scorecard (Step 5)

Interviews are scored, not vibes-based. The example uses a **two-round structured scorecard**, each round scored across five areas on a 0–3 scale (0 = not demonstrated, 1 = below, 2 = meets, 3 = exceeds), for a combined total across both interviews **plus** the task.

**Round 1 — First Round** (the craft): Technical skills & software · Platform & content awareness · Creative eye & storytelling · Speed & efficiency · Industry/creator-economy awareness. *(/15)*

**Final Round** (fit + depth): Motivation & culture fit · Communication & collaboration · Strategic thinking · Edit-task walkthrough (defend the decisions in the task they submitted) · Organisation & process. *(/15)*

**Combined: Interview 1 + Interview 2 + Task = /25**, with a clear recommendation (Strong Yes → progress to offer / No → not right now).

> **Why score it:** a rubric makes interviews consistent across interviewers and candidates, forces you to assess the dimensions that actually matter (not just likeability), and creates a defensible record. Note the *edit-task walkthrough* round — the candidate defends the §3 task, which tests whether they can reason about their own work, not just produce it.

### Good vs bad remote employee — the signals
Because most of these roles are remote, the materials include a behavioural checklist that surfaces during task/interview/trial:

| ✅ Good remote employee | ⛔️ Bad remote employee |
|---|---|
| Quick response to messages | Overly enthusiastic in interview (verging on cheesy) |
| Proactively shares updates | Camera off — are they engaging? |
| Shows passion for the industry | Slow/no response (maybe working a second job) |
| Delivers high-quality work consistently | Quality far below standard |
| Deep understanding of their field | No proactive updates |
| Readily available when needed | Fails to improve on feedback |
| Meets deadlines without reminders | Sick days as soon as they join |
| Strong sense of accountability | Too many Qs on basic software (no initiative) |
| Adapts well to changing priorities | Doesn't ask questions / seek clarification at all |
| Seeks feedback and acts on it promptly | Too many excuses for unavailability ("internet!") |

> **The throughline of the "good" column:** responsiveness, proactivity, accountability, and acting on feedback. These are *operating behaviours*, not skills — which is exactly why the **2-week trial (Step 6)** exists: skills show up in the task, but these behaviours only show up in real working conditions.

## 6. Trial + Contract (Steps 6–7)

- **2-week trial** confirms work ethic and output under real conditions before permanent commitment — the final, behavioural filter that the task can't capture.
- **Contract** on passing. The example **Freelancer / Consultant & Contractor Agreement** is a full deed; a clause-by-clause map of what such an agreement typically covers:

| Clause area | What it governs (in plain terms) |
|---|---|
| **Term & engagement** | Start date; rolling until terminated; notice period (e.g. 7 days). |
| **Provision of services** | Duty of care/skill; devote agreed time; comply with laws and company policies (IT, data, anti-harassment, etc.); no authority to bind the company unless authorised. |
| **Anti-bribery / anti-tax-evasion** | Compliance with the Bribery Act 2010 and Criminal Finances Act 2017; report demands; breach = immediate termination. |
| **Fees & expenses** | Day/hour rate ex-VAT; monthly invoice; payment terms (e.g. 7 days); freelancer bears own expenses. |
| **Other activities** | May work elsewhere *unless* it competes or breaches obligations; company work gets priority. |
| **Confidential information** | Broad definition (client lists, financials, strategy, launch dates); keep secret during and after; return/delete on exit; no social-media disclosure of client work. |
| **Intellectual property** | All work product & inventions assigned to the company; moral rights waived; cooperate to register/defend IP. |
| **Data protection** | Comply with UK GDPR/DPA 2018; personal liability + indemnity for breaches. |
| **Insurance & liability** | Indemnify the company for losses from breach/negligence; maintain adequate insurance. |
| **Termination** | Immediate for material breach, gross misconduct, policy breach, bribery/tax offences, fraud, incapacity, insolvency, etc. |
| **Obligations on termination** | Return all company property/confidential info; delete stored data (incl. business contacts from personal accounts). |
| **Restrictive covenants** | Time-bound non-solicit of customers/staff and non-compete after termination; non-disparagement. |
| **Status** | Independent contractor — not an employee/worker/partner; freelancer responsible for own tax/NI. |
| **General** | Entire agreement, notices, variation in writing, governing law (England & Wales), executed as a deed; **Schedule 1** lists the specific role & responsibilities. |

> **Not legal advice.** This is a plain-language overview of what a contractor agreement commonly contains, to help you read one — it isn't legal advice and I'm not a lawyer. Employment/contractor law, IR35-style status rules, restrictive-covenant enforceability, and IP/tax treatment vary by jurisdiction and by how someone actually works. Have any agreement reviewed by a qualified lawyer in the relevant country before using it, and take advice on worker-status classification.

## 7. Team Onboarding (once hired)

Hiring ends; onboarding begins. A 7-step process that mirrors *Client* Onboarding but pointed inward at a new team member:

1. **Contracts filed + HR system set up** — contract signed → everything securely filed.
2. **Welcome email** — sent so they feel the process is professional and have a plan from day one.
3. **Personal details saved** — obtain passport + proof of address, verified over a virtual call.
4. **Task list set** — curated in the PM system (Asana) for what they need to learn and execute in their first weeks.
5. **Intro meetings** — with relevant team members so they feel part of the team and learn who does what / who to go to for what.
6. **Test meeting** — manager checks in on how they're getting on and *subtly tests* whether they've digested the onboarding information.
7. **Work + continue trial** — they continue through their onboarding checklist and execute work as normal from here.

**The welcome email** (Step 2) is itself a template: warm welcome → start date → **email/account setup** (credentials to be reset on first login) → invitations to the core systems (**Asana** = tasks + their onboarding list, **Slack** = comms with photo/title, **HR system** e.g. Hibob = people management) → **first-day plan** (online by 9am, run through onboarding, then manager connects).

> **The parallel to Client Onboarding is deliberate:** both alternate setup steps with relationship/expectation steps, both run on a checklist in the PM system, and both aim to make the new party (client *or* teammate) feel the operation is organised and professional from day one. The new hire's onboarding task list lives on the same Asana backbone as the client delivery boards from File 3.

> **AI-native parallel:** team onboarding maps almost perfectly onto **deploying an agent**: "personal details / credentials" → provisioning the agent's access and tools; "task list set in Asana" → loading the agent's initial workflows; "intro meetings — who to go to for what" → giving the agent its routing/escalation map; "test meeting — did they digest the info" → a validation eval before live work; "continue trial" → monitored rollout. Onboarding a teammate and standing up an agent are the same shape: provision → equip → orient → validate → supervised live.

## 8. Training & Performance — the review process

Development is continuous, anchored by a structured **performance review (an 11-step process)**:

1. **Draft review forms** — a form each employee *and* each manager completes before the review meeting, to gather information/feedback and get the most out of the meeting.
2. **Team communication** — share the forms by email; communicate the completion deadline.
3. **Meetings set** — managers schedule the review meetings in employees' calendars.
4. *(Prep)* — review performance, prep feedback and improvements, understand the status of the OKRs each person owns.
5. **Salary / role adjustments** — each manager outlines requested salary/role/responsibility adjustments *with reasoning.*
6. **Salary/role adjustments confirmed** — the founder reviews and approves/declines each, with reasoning.
7. **Review forms delivered** — sent ~48 hours before meetings so managers can digest them.
8. **Managers' review meetings** — meet with managers first; discuss their own performance and their departments in detail, which feeds into their team meetings.
9. **Employee review meetings** — 45–60 mins; a *constructive* conversation (coaching and realigning), **not** just reading the form aloud.
10. **Goals shared** — manager follows up in writing with any new goals set for the employee.
11. **Goals tracked** — new goals added to the PM system (Asana) to be tracked and reviewed over the next 6 months.

> **The design:** reviews are *prepared* (forms both ways, delivered 48h ahead), *cascaded* (managers reviewed first so they can run their teams' reviews well), *decisive* (salary/role changes proposed with reasoning and approved at founder level), and *forward-looking* (it ends in tracked goals on the same Asana backbone, reviewed over 6 months — not a one-off chat). The explicit "shouldn't just read the form" note keeps the meeting a coaching conversation.

> **AI-native parallel — and its limit.** The review *loop* maps onto how you'd maintain an agent: evaluate against goals/OKRs, give corrective feedback, set new targets, track over time — continuous evals are a performance-review cadence for agents. **But the human core doesn't transfer:** salary/role/coaching/motivation/career growth are about people, and an AI-native agency still does this for its (smaller, higher-leverage) human team. The "good remote employee" behaviours from §5 — accountability, acting on feedback, proactivity — are precisely the human qualities you're hiring *for* in the roles you keep human.

## Synthesis — how Hiring & Training fits the knowledge base

Building the team is a single filtered pipeline plus an ongoing development loop:

1. **Hire as a filter funnel** (§1): JD → advertise + form → **task** → shortlist on the task → scored interview → **2-week trial** → contract. Each step de-risks before a bigger investment; the task and the trial are the load-bearing filters.
2. **Specific JDs + role-specific tasks** (§2–3) self-select people who can do the *actual* work; **the right board** (§4) matches talent tier and contract type to the role.
3. **Structured scorecards + remote-behaviour signals** (§5) make interviews consistent and surface the operating behaviours that only show up under real conditions.
4. **Trial → contract** (§6) confirms behaviour before commitment; the agreement covers services, confidentiality, IP, data, restrictive covenants, and status (overview only — get legal advice).
5. **Team onboarding** (§7) mirrors client onboarding on the same PM backbone, and **performance reviews** (§8) close the loop with prepared, cascaded, decisive, forward-looking development.

**The single biggest idea:** hire on *demonstrated ability under real conditions* (task → trial), not on CVs and charisma — and treat training as a continuous, tracked loop, not an event. **For an AI-native agency**, this file is where the human/agent line is drawn most sharply: you still hire and develop people — *fewer of them, for the judgement/taste/accountability/relationship gates that shouldn't be automated* — while the very same discipline (spec → eval → supervised trial → onboard → review) is, structurally, how you safely stand up and maintain an agent. Hiring well and deploying agents well turn out to be the same craft pointed at different kinds of worker; the irreducibly human part is the coaching, motivation, and growth of the people you keep.
