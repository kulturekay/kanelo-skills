# Sign Clients Like Never Before

> **Section 4 of the "How to Build a Service-Based Business" knowledge base.**
> Sections 1–3 built the foundation: *what* you sell (Service & Niche), *who* you sell to and *what you say* (Niche & Target Market), and *how you price it* (High-Converting Offers). This section is the **client-acquisition engine** — the end-to-end pipeline that turns a name on a list into a signed client.

The pipeline has four stages: **Qualify → Contact → Pitch → Close.** One principle runs through all of them: **lead with the client's problem, and withhold price until your value is undeniable.**

---

## Stage 1 — Lead Analysis (qualify *before* you chase)

Before spending effort on any lead, score it. Chasing fake or bad-fit leads is the biggest waste of an agency's time. Every lead falls into one of four buckets:

| Bucket | What it is | Pursue? |
|---|---|---|
| **Fake** | Looks like a business but isn't real/viable | ❌ No |
| **Good** | Legit, established, real budget and presence | ✅ Yes |
| **Bad** | Real, but a poor fit (wrong industry, no budget, fundamental issues) | ❌ Usually no |
| **High Potential** | Fast-growing, strong brand/founder, room to scale, clear solvable problems | ✅✅ Priority |

### The Lead Analysis worksheet

For each lead, answer:

1. **Client name.**
2. **IACP?** — Do they fit your ideal profile topline? (Yes/No — see Section 2.)
3. **How legit are they?** — Check the website, find the founder, check company *and* founder socials, check LinkedIn, Google them.
4. **Size of the business** — Check web traffic, employee count, your own industry tools, signs of success.
5. **Who is the lead person?** — What's the role of the person who got in touch (or who you'd contact)?
6. **What problem do they want solved?** — Reuse your IACP work; guess what they *really* want.
7. **What service do they want?** — List every service they'd want from you.
8. **What offer would work?** — A rough monthly figure based on all of the above.

### How to tell the buckets apart (from the worked examples)

- **Fake signals:** no founder info; repeated stock images; products *always* "sold out"; tiny or questionable engagement (e.g. 380 followers, last post 1.5 years ago); no reviews; one generic contact form for everything; design flaws, spelling errors, unrealistic claims. *Estimated offer: ~$200/m — not worth it.*
- **Good signals:** professional site and clear product offering; specific positive reviews/testimonials; active blog/content; strong active socials; press mentions; an identifiable, credible founder. *(e.g. Savills, Jot, Blume.)*
- **Bad signals:** real but unfit — wrong industry, tiny solopreneur with no budget, no social proof, no ecom functionality, outdated UX, a fundamental product issue. *(e.g. a generic mindset coach, Morty & Bob's, Bishopston Hardware.)*
- **High-Potential signals:** fast-growing, strong brand identity, big or rapidly growing audience, raised funding / high revenue, high LTV (e.g. subscription model), clear room to expand, *and* clear problems you can solve. *(e.g. Jade Beason, Magic Spoon, Feastables.)* *Estimated offers: $7–10k/m+.*

> **The payoff:** spend your outreach energy on **High Potential** first, then **Good**. Quietly drop **Fake** and **Bad**. The worksheet is essentially your IACP from Section 2 turned into a scoring rubric for the leads you actually find.

## Stage 2 — Outreach (earn the reply)

Two modes, depending on whether you're going 1:1 or at scale.

### Hot Outreach — personalised, researched, 1:1

A three-section structure that leads with *their* problem and gives value before asking for anything:

**Section 1 — Peak Interest (the problem you found)**
1. State the problem you spotted — *"your podcast clips are edited poorly," "your current funnel is flawed."*
2. Mention something non-obvious about them — *"I listened to what you said in X," "you have a TikTok account but no TikTok Shop," "you're not using email automations."*
3. Add a genuine connection — *"I also love X place," "that UFC fight yesterday was amazing."*

**Section 2 — The Value (what makes you an expert)**
Give them the answer *right now*. Whatever you'd want them to pay for, hand them a piece of it free:
- *"Switch your email to long-form and you'll easily see a 20% open-rate difference."*
- *"I re-edited one of your videos — here it is, and here's why I did XYZ."*
- *"Based on your engagement and traffic, a TikTok Shop would likely sell ~2k orders/month."*

**Section 3 — About you + CTA**
- *"I'm [X] from [Y]. I've done [Z]. I think I can help you here."*
- **Always end with a question** — never *"let me know"* or *"let's get a call."* Ask: *"What do you think of your short-form content?"* / *"Have you bought anything from TikTok Shop?"*

**Worked example (paid-ads agency → a brand not running ads):**
> *"Hi X, I bought your T-shirt and love the material — been wearing it constantly. I was browsing the site and noticed you don't run any ads. I think you could kill it with some UGC showing the product being worn, as it sells itself. I know this because I run an agency that does this for brands — done it for X, Y, Z. On average we see cost-per-acquisition around $7, which I think would help you scale. Have you run ads, or is there a reason you haven't started?"*

**Hot follow-up angles** (same structure, rotate the narrative):
Problem → Solution · Opportunity → Money · Personal connection → Trust · Highlight competitors → "you can do this too."

### Cold Outreach — automated to a pulled list

Define tight targeting criteria, then send a templated (but still personalised-by-merge-field) message. Example targeting for a paid-ads play:
- D2C brand · **>10k visits/month** · **>10 employees on LinkedIn** · **problem: not running ads (or doing them poorly).**

**Template:**
> *"Hi {{firstName}}, came across {{companyName}} last night while shopping. Love the messaging and what you're doing. I noticed you don't use many paid ads. I run agency X — I think organic-style try-on videos, unboxings, and product reviews would do really well for you. Would love to help you scale. What's the current strategy?"*

Two more worked examples in the materials: a **CRO-agency founder** pitched on building a personal brand ("you used to post but went inactive — massive missed opportunity… here's an example… 2 hours/week for 7 posts"), and a **D2C clothing brand** pitched on better ad scripting ("got hit with your ad… I'd change the format to an agreeable-statement hook… what's your plan for paid media going into Q4?").

**Cold follow-up cadence:**
- **Day 2** — continue the same messaging angle.
- **Day 5** — a *new* angle / fresh email.
- **Day 10** — continue the Day-5 angle.

**Never say** (in any follow-up): *"just following up," "did you get a chance to check this," "just wanted to know if you're interested."*

> **The outreach throughline:** make it about *them*, give value upfront, prove expertise with a specific insight, and end with a real question. That's what separates a reply from the ignore pile.

## Stage 3 — The Pitch Deck

Once outreach earns a call, the deck carries the pitch.

**Goal:** a clean, visually appealing deck — **10–20 slides max** unless more are truly necessary.

| Section | Slides | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Cover** | 1 | Clean and sharp; add the client's logo to personalise. |
| **Agency Overview** | 2–3 | Briefly introduce your agency; snapshot of past clients and wins — big highlights, no fluff. |
| **Client Highlight** | 1 | Acknowledge something genuinely great about the client — authentic and relevant. |
| **Problem Discovery** | 2–3 | Clearly outline the main issues; make them resonate with what the client already suspects. |
| **Solution Proposal** | 2–3 | Your plan for each problem — specific, achievable solutions. |
| **Value-Driven Examples** | 3–4 | Case studies, examples, success stories — prove your impact. |
| **Risk Mitigation** | 1–2 | Address concerns about working with you; show you've already planned for them. |
| **Process & Timeline** | 1–2 | A brief implementation roadmap with timelines and touchpoints — *process, not price*. |

**Design guidelines:**
- **Keep it simple** — clean backgrounds, consistent fonts, minimal text; let visuals and data tell the story.
- **No price tags or generic info** — anything one-size-fits-all dilutes the message.
- **Options vs. single solutions** — present more than one approach where it applies; it builds flexibility and keeps the client engaged.

> **The golden rule:** the deck never shows price. Its job is to make the problem undeniable and your ability to solve it obvious.

## Stage 4 — Sales Calls

Two calls: the first sells *value*, the second introduces *price* and closes.

### First call — establish value (no price)
- Be the **first to join**. Say hello.
- **Introduce yourself in 60 seconds**, then listen as they introduce themselves.
- State the call's objective — keep it **client-focused.**
- **Share your screen (tab only)** — don't expose other content. Keep **their face visible** while presenting.
- **Guide them through the slides — explain, don't read.** Stop at natural points to spark conversation. If it's flowing, be confident and just talk. Re-share slides as needed.
- **Focus on value, avoid price.** Make it about how good you are.
- **Follow up by email** with the deck and a brief call summary. Propose times for a second call, and give them something to think about in the meantime.

### Second call — price tied to ROI, then close
- Open with **small talk** to warm up.
- Say you've **been thinking about and working on their project.**
- Ask for **feedback** on the first call.
- **Present slides** of what you can do for them; **list the specific services.**
- Describe how **communication and workflow** will work.
- **Share pricing — explicitly linked to the ROI** they'll get.
- Open **Q&A** to handle concerns.
- If they're ready, **send the agreement and next steps.**

> **The throughline:** value is fully established before price ever appears — same logic as the deck. By the time you quote, the client is sold on the outcome, so the price is framed as the cost of a return, not an expense.

## Synthesis — how this section connects

The acquisition engine runs on everything built earlier:

1. **Qualify (Lead Analysis)** — score leads against your **IACP** (Section 2). Prioritise High-Potential and Good; drop Fake and Bad. Each lead's "problem" and "offer" columns are your IACP and pricing work (Sections 2–3) applied to a real prospect.
2. **Contact (Outreach)** — lead with their **problem** (Section 1's Offer→Problem framing), give value upfront, prove expertise, end with a question. Hot for 1:1, cold for scale, with disciplined follow-up cadences.
3. **Pitch (Deck)** — Problem Discovery → Solution → Value-Driven Examples mirrors the **Claim → Approach → Proof** messaging from Section 2, rendered as slides. No price.
4. **Close (Sales Calls)** — value first, then price framed against ROI — exactly how the **Base + Performance** offers (Section 3) are justified.

**The single biggest idea:** the entire machine is engineered to make price the *last* thing the client thinks about. Every stage leads with the client's problem and stacks proof of value — so by the time money comes up, the only question left is *when to start*, not *whether it's worth it*.
